Unit 1 · 8–10% of Exam

The Global Tapestry

c. 1200–1450

⚖️ Exam weight: 8–10%

By 1200 CE, powerful civilizations thrived on every continent — largely disconnected from each other. This unit zooms into six regions: East Asia, the Islamic world, South & Southeast Asia, the Americas, Africa, and Europe, showing how each built states, organized societies, and practiced religion.

1.1Developments in East Asia+

The Song Dynasty (960–1279) made China the most advanced economy in the world. New rice varieties, an expanded canal system, and a growing merchant class fueled a commercial revolution. Confucian ideas — especially the civil service exam — shaped who held power.

Key Vocabulary
Song Dynasty
Chinese dynasty (960–1279) known for economic growth, printing, gunpowder, and the compass.
Neo-Confucianism
An updated Confucian philosophy blending Buddhist and Taoist ideas; emphasized social harmony and loyalty to hierarchy.
Civil Service Exam
A test based on Confucian texts determining who could work in government — allowed talented men (not just nobles) to rise.
Champa Rice
A fast-growing rice from Vietnam allowing two harvests per year, leading to massive population growth in China.
Grand Canal
An enormous waterway connecting northern and southern China, enabling cheap transport of goods.
Foot Binding
A painful practice applied to women's feet in Song China that symbolized high status but severely limited mobility.
🎯 Exam TipThe Song Dynasty is a go-to example for economic and technological innovation. On essays, connect it to trade networks in Unit 2 — the Silk Road and Indian Ocean trade carried Chinese innovations outward.
1.2Developments in Dar al-Islam+

Dar al-Islam ('the house of Islam') refers to the vast world connected by Islamic faith — stretching from Spain to Southeast Asia. By 1200, the Abbasid Caliphate was crumbling, but new Turkic-led states stepped in. Despite political fragmentation, Islam kept the region culturally and intellectually united.

Key Vocabulary
Abbasid Caliphate
Major Islamic empire centered in Baghdad (750–1258); a hub of trade, mathematics, and learning — the Islamic Golden Age.
Seljuk Turks
Turkic people who converted to Islam and took over large parts of Abbasid territory, controlling Baghdad while allowing the Caliph as a figurehead.
Delhi Sultanate
An Islamic state in northern India (1206–1526) established by Turkic rulers, blending Persian, Turkic, and Indian cultures.
Mamluks
Former enslaved soldiers who became rulers of Egypt. They defeated the Mongols at Ain Jalut (1260) — a turning point in Islamic history.
House of Wisdom
A great intellectual center in Baghdad where scholars translated Greek, Persian, and Indian texts, advancing math, medicine, and astronomy.
Sufism
A mystical branch of Islam focused on personal spiritual connection to God. Sufi missionaries helped spread Islam peacefully across Africa and Asia.
🎯 Exam TipDon't confuse political fragmentation with cultural collapse. Dar al-Islam stayed connected through trade, pilgrimage (Hajj), and shared language (Arabic). This nuance earns points on essays.
1.3South and Southeast Asia+

South and Southeast Asia were hotspots of religious competition. Hinduism, Buddhism, and Islam competed for followers — sometimes peacefully, sometimes in tension. Powerful kingdoms like the Khmer Empire in Cambodia show how faiths shaped art, architecture, and politics.

Key Vocabulary
Bhakti Movement
A Hindu devotional movement emphasizing personal connection to God — open to all castes including women, challenging rigid hierarchy.
Khmer Empire
A powerful Hindu-Buddhist kingdom in Southeast Asia, famous for building Angkor Wat — one of the largest religious monuments ever built.
Caste System
The Hindu social hierarchy dividing people by birth (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra). Determined job, marriage, and social status.
Theravada Buddhism
A conservative form of Buddhism that spread widely in mainland Southeast Asia, emphasizing personal salvation through monastic life.
🎯 Exam TipSoutheast Asia is a great example to show how trade spread religion — merchants carried both Islam and Buddhism along Indian Ocean routes into the region.
1.4State Building in the Americas+

Completely isolated from Afro-Eurasia, the Americas developed sophisticated civilizations independently. The Aztec and Inca empires were the most powerful, but cultures like the Mississippians in North America also built complex societies with trade, religion, and agriculture.

Key Vocabulary
Aztec Empire
A powerful Mesoamerican empire centered at Tenochtitlán. Built on tribute from conquered peoples and featured elaborate religious ceremonies.
Inca Empire
The largest empire in pre-Columbian Americas, centered in the Andes. Controlled through a road system, mit'a labor, and quipu record-keeping.
Mit'a
An Inca labor system requiring citizens to work for the state a certain number of days per year — building roads, temples, and farming state lands.
Cahokia
A Mississippian city near modern St. Louis with a population of up to 20,000 — larger than London in 1200 CE. Known for massive earthen mounds.
🎯 Exam TipThe Americas are often overlooked but appear on DBQs and LEQs. Use the Aztec or Inca as examples of complex state-building that happened independently of Afro-Eurasia.
1.5State Building in Africa+

Africa was home to wealthy and powerful kingdoms long before European contact. The Mali Empire controlled vast gold and salt trade routes. Great Zimbabwe was a major trading hub. These states show that Africa had complex political and economic systems.

Key Vocabulary
Mali Empire
A West African empire (c. 1235–1600) controlling trans-Saharan gold and salt trade. Its ruler Mansa Musa was the wealthiest person in the world.
Mansa Musa
King of Mali whose 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca (with 60,000 people and vast gold) put Mali on the world map and caused inflation across North Africa.
Great Zimbabwe
A stone-walled city in southern Africa serving as a center of trade, connecting the African interior with Indian Ocean merchants.
Griots
Oral historians and storytellers in West African societies who preserved history, laws, and culture through spoken word — passed down for generations.
Swahili Coast
The East African coastline where Bantu-speaking Africans mixed with Arab traders, creating a unique Swahili culture and thriving port cities.
1.6Developments in Europe+

Medieval Europe was politically fragmented but not stagnant. Feudalism organized society around land and military service. The Catholic Church was the most powerful unifying force. By the 1200s, universities were spreading, trade was growing, and monarchs were slowly centralizing power.

Key Vocabulary
Feudalism
A system where kings granted land (fiefs) to lords for military service. Lords gave land to knights; peasants (serfs) worked the land in exchange for protection.
Magna Carta (1215)
A document signed by King John of England limiting royal power and guaranteeing rights to nobles — an early step toward constitutional government.
Crusades
Religious wars (1095–1291) in which European Christians tried to recapture Jerusalem from Muslim rulers. Had major effects on trade and cultural exchange.
Byzantine Empire
The continuation of the eastern Roman Empire, centered in Constantinople — a Christian empire bridging Europe and the Islamic world until 1453.
1.7Comparison: c. 1200–1450+

A key AP skill is comparing how different regions built states. Look for patterns: What role did religion play in legitimizing rulers? How did rulers extract resources? How did trade connect societies?

Key Vocabulary
Similarities
Religion legitimized rulers everywhere: the Pope in Europe, Islam in Dar al-Islam, Confucianism in China, Hinduism/Buddhism in South and Southeast Asia.
Key Difference
China had a centralized bureaucracy based on merit (civil service exams), while Europe and Japan had decentralized feudal systems based on hereditary land grants.
🎯 Exam TipUnit 1.7 is designed for comparison essays (LEQs). Practice comparing two regions on a specific topic: state-building, role of women, or religious influence.
1.8Unit 1 Overview: Putting It Together+

By 1450, you should be able to describe major political, cultural, and economic developments in six world regions — and compare them. The big takeaway: diverse civilizations developed complex systems independently, but were increasingly connected through trade, religion, and migration. That connectivity explodes in Unit 2.

🎯 Exam TipKey Themes to Remember: State-building · Religion as a social force · Gender roles · Economic systems · Cultural diffusion. These themes repeat across ALL 9 units.
Unit 2 · 8–10% of Exam

Networks of Exchange

c. 1200–1450

⚖️ Exam weight: 8–10%

This unit is about how the world became more connected between 1200 and 1450. The Silk Roads, Indian Ocean trade, and Trans-Saharan routes carried not just goods, but also religions, diseases, ideas, and technology across thousands of miles. The Mongol Empire played a huge role in making overland trade safer and faster.

2.1The Silk Roads+

The Silk Roads were a vast network of overland and sea routes connecting China to the Mediterranean. They carried silk, spices, horses, glassware, paper, and diseases. Merchants, missionaries, and diplomats all traveled these routes, spreading culture and religion as they went.

Key Vocabulary
Silk Roads
A network of trade routes connecting East Asia, Central Asia, South Asia, East Africa, and Europe. Named for Chinese silk, but carried a huge variety of goods and ideas.
Caravanserai
Roadside inns spaced a day's journey apart along the Silk Roads where merchants could rest, trade, and exchange information safely.
Pax Mongolica
'Mongol Peace' — a period of relative stability across the Mongol Empire (c.1250–1350) that made overland trade safer and more efficient than ever before.
🎯 Exam TipThe Silk Roads are one of the most-tested topics in AP World. Always explain WHAT moved along them — goods, religion, disease, technology — not just that they existed.
2.2The Mongol Empire+

The Mongols built the largest contiguous land empire in history under Genghis Khan and his descendants. They were terrifyingly effective conquerors, but once in control, they generally allowed conquered peoples to keep their religions and cultures.

Key Vocabulary
Genghis Khan
Founder of the Mongol Empire (r. 1206–1227), who united the Mongol tribes and launched conquests across Central Asia, China, Persia, and Russia.
Kublai Khan
Genghis Khan's grandson who conquered the Song Dynasty and founded the Yuan Dynasty in China, ruling as Mongol Emperor (r. 1271–1294).
Yuan Dynasty
The Mongol-ruled dynasty in China (1271–1368). Despite being foreigners, the Mongols largely maintained the Chinese bureaucracy and promoted trade.
Il-Khanate
The Mongol state that ruled Persia. The Il-Khans eventually converted to Islam, showing how conquerors were often absorbed into local cultures.
🎯 Exam TipThe Mongols are complex — they destroyed cities but also facilitated trade. Graders reward essays that show BOTH sides: destruction AND the Pax Mongolica that followed.
2.3Exchange in the Indian Ocean+

The Indian Ocean trade network was the busiest and most profitable in the world during this period. Merchants used monsoon winds to sail predictable routes connecting East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China. This network spread Islam and made the region enormously wealthy.

Key Vocabulary
Monsoon Winds
Seasonal winds in the Indian Ocean that blow northeast in winter and southwest in summer, allowing sailors to predict and plan voyages.
Dhow
A sailing ship with triangular (lateen) sails used by Arab merchants across the Indian Ocean. Designed to take advantage of monsoon winds.
Ibn Battuta
A Moroccan Muslim traveler who journeyed 75,000 miles across the Islamic world and beyond (1325–1354), leaving detailed accounts of societies he visited.
Swahili City-States
Prosperous trading cities along East Africa's coast (Kilwa, Mombasa, Zanzibar) where African and Arab cultures mixed to create the Swahili language.
2.4Trans-Saharan Trade+

Camel caravans crossed the Sahara Desert to connect West Africa's gold and salt to North Africa and the Mediterranean. This trade made the Mali Empire fabulously wealthy and spread Islam deep into West Africa. Salt was so valuable it was literally traded for gold by weight.

Key Vocabulary
Trans-Saharan Trade
Trade routes crossing the Sahara Desert connecting West Africa to North Africa, primarily trading gold, salt, enslaved people, and kola nuts.
Camels
The 'ships of the desert' — camels made trans-Saharan trade possible by storing water and carrying heavy loads across thousands of miles of desert.
Timbuktu
A major city in the Mali Empire that was both a trade hub and a center of Islamic learning, home to the Sankore University and thousands of scholars.
2.5Cultural Effects of Trade+

Trade didn't just move goods — it spread religions, languages, technologies, and artistic styles. Buddhism spread along the Silk Roads. Islam spread along both the Silk Roads and Indian Ocean. Wherever merchants went, culture followed.

Key Vocabulary
Cultural Diffusion
The spread of cultural elements from one society to another through contact — trade routes were the main highway for cultural diffusion.
Diasporic Communities
Communities of merchants who settled permanently in foreign cities to facilitate trade, like Chinese merchants in Southeast Asia or Muslim merchants in Indian ports.
2.6Environmental Effects of Trade+

Trade networks also spread deadly disease. The Black Death traveled from Central Asia along Mongol trade routes to reach China, the Middle East, and Europe between the 1330s and 1350s, killing up to one-third of Europe's population and reshaping societies everywhere it struck.

Key Vocabulary
Black Death
A devastating bubonic plague pandemic that swept Eurasia in the 1340s–50s. Spread via fleas on rats along trade routes. Killed 30–60% of Europe's population.
Bubonic Plague
A bacterial disease caused by Yersinia pestis, spread by flea bites. Symptoms included swollen lymph nodes (buboes), fever, and rapid death.
🎯 Exam TipThe Black Death is a perfect example to show how trade had unintended consequences. Great for DBQs about trade or environmental change.
2.7Comparison in Trade c. 1200–1450+

Compare the three major trade networks: the Silk Roads (overland, Central Asia), the Indian Ocean (maritime, monsoons), and the Trans-Saharan routes (camel caravans, Sahara). Each had different geography, goods, and cultural impacts — but all spread Islam and connected distant civilizations.

Key Vocabulary
Silk Roads vs. Indian Ocean
Silk Roads = overland, riskier, slower; Indian Ocean = maritime, faster, higher volume. Both carried luxury goods and spread religion, but Indian Ocean trade was more profitable by 1450.
Role of Islam
Muslim merchants dominated all three networks. Islam spread along trade routes because merchants shared faith and used Islamic banking and law to facilitate deals.
Unit 3 · 12–15% of Exam

Land-Based Empires

c. 1450–1750

⚖️ Exam weight: 12–15%

Between 1450 and 1750, powerful empires dominated huge stretches of land using new gunpowder weapons. The Ottomans, Safavids, Mughals, and others are called 'gunpowder empires' because cannons and firearms helped them conquer and control large territories. These empires were multicultural, religiously complex, and commercially sophisticated.

3.1Expansion of Land-Based Empires+

Gunpowder technology — developed in China but spread via trade — allowed empires to conquer and hold territory in unprecedented ways. Cannons could blast through medieval walls. Firearms gave infantry an advantage over cavalry. This 'military revolution' changed the balance of power globally.

Key Vocabulary
Gunpowder Empires
A term for empires (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Russian, Chinese) that used firearms and cannons to conquer large land areas between 1450–1750.
Ottoman Empire
A massive Sunni Muslim empire (1299–1922) centered in modern Turkey. Conquered Constantinople in 1453, controlled trade routes, and ruled from the Balkans to Arabia.
Conquest of Constantinople (1453)
Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II used massive cannons to breach Constantinople's walls, ending the Byzantine Empire and making Istanbul the new Ottoman capital.
Safavid Empire
A Shia Muslim empire in Persia (modern Iran), ruling 1501–1736. Fierce rivals of the Sunni Ottomans, leading to constant warfare.
Mughal Empire
A Muslim dynasty ruling most of South Asia (1526–1857). Founded by Babur, the Mughals blended Persian, Turkic, and Indian culture and built the Taj Mahal.
Russian Empire
Expanding eastward across Siberia under Ivan the Terrible and later Peter the Great, Russia used gunpowder weapons to conquer nomadic peoples and build a massive empire.
3.2Governments of Land-Based Empires+

These empires all needed ways to administer vast, diverse populations. They developed bureaucracies, used taxation, built roads, and often employed religious institutions to help govern. Many used enslaved or captured people in their militaries and administrations.

Key Vocabulary
Devshirme System
An Ottoman practice of taking Christian boys from conquered territories, converting them to Islam, and training them as elite soldiers (Janissaries) or government officials.
Janissaries
Elite Ottoman infantry soldiers, recruited through the devshirme system. Loyal directly to the Sultan and the backbone of the Ottoman army.
Akbar the Great
Mughal Emperor (r. 1556–1605) famous for religious tolerance, welcoming Hindus, Christians, and Zoroastrians into his court and government.
Millet System
An Ottoman system allowing non-Muslim communities (Jews, Christians, Armenians) to govern themselves under their own religious laws within the empire.
3.3Belief Systems of Land-Based Empires+

Religion was central to how these empires legitimized power. The Ottoman sultan claimed the title of Caliph (leader of all Sunni Muslims). The Safavids made Shia Islam the state religion. The Mughals' relationship with Hinduism shaped Indian history for centuries.

Key Vocabulary
Sunni-Shia Split
The major division in Islam over who should lead after Muhammad's death. Ottomans = Sunni; Safavids = Shia. Their rivalry was partly religious, partly political.
Sikhism
A religion founded in the Punjab in the 15th century by Guru Nanak, blending elements of Islam and Hinduism, emphasizing equality and one God.
3.4Unit 3 Comparison & Wrap-Up+

All land-based empires used similar strategies: military conquest with gunpowder, bureaucratic administration, and religious legitimacy. But they differed in how they treated religious minorities, organized their armies, and handled succession.

🎯 Exam TipThis is a HIGH-priority unit (12–15%). Compare the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires on: (1) religious policy, (2) military organization, (3) treatment of non-Muslims. These comparisons appear frequently on LEQs and DBQs.
Unit 4 · 12–15% of Exam

Transoceanic Interconnections

c. 1450–1750

⚖️ Exam weight: 12–15%

After 1492, the world changed forever. European voyages to the Americas, Africa, and Asia created truly global trade networks for the first time. This unit covers European exploration, the Columbian Exchange, the Atlantic slave trade, and how these developments transformed economies and societies on every continent.

4.1Technological Innovations and Exploration+

European exploration was made possible by new navigation technologies — many borrowed from other cultures. The magnetic compass came from China. The lateen sail came from Arab traders. The astrolabe came from Islamic astronomers. Europeans combined these tools to sail routes no one had navigated before.

Key Vocabulary
Caravel
A small, maneuverable Portuguese sailing ship with lateen sails that could sail against the wind — essential for exploring the African coast and Atlantic Ocean.
Astrolabe
A navigation tool that measured the angle of stars above the horizon, allowing sailors to calculate their latitude at sea.
Prince Henry the Navigator
A Portuguese prince who sponsored systematic exploration of the African coast beginning in the 1420s, establishing Portugal as the first major European maritime power.
4.2Exploration: Causes and Events+

Why did Europeans explore? Three main motivations: God (spreading Christianity), Glory (national prestige), and Gold (profit). The fall of Constantinople in 1453 blocked overland trade routes to Asia, giving Europeans extra incentive to find sea routes.

Key Vocabulary
Columbus (1492)
Christopher Columbus, sailing for Spain, reached the Caribbean in 1492 — beginning sustained contact between Europe and the Americas, with enormous global consequences.
Vasco da Gama
Portuguese explorer who sailed around Africa to reach India in 1498, opening a direct sea route between Europe and Asia that bypassed Islamic middlemen.
Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)
An agreement between Spain and Portugal dividing the non-European world — Portugal got Africa/Asia, Spain got the Americas.
4.3The Columbian Exchange+

The Columbian Exchange was the massive two-way transfer of plants, animals, diseases, and people between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia after 1492. It was one of the most consequential ecological events in human history.

Key Vocabulary
Columbian Exchange
The transfer of plants, animals, diseases, and culture between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia after Columbus's 1492 voyage. Changed diets and populations globally.
New World to Old World
Potatoes, corn, tomatoes, cacao, tobacco, and rubber went to Europe, Africa, and Asia — dramatically increasing calorie availability and fueling population growth.
Old World to New World
Horses, cattle, pigs, wheat, and — most devastatingly — diseases (smallpox, measles) went to the Americas. Indigenous populations fell by up to 90%.
Demographic Collapse
The catastrophic population decline of Indigenous Americans after European contact, primarily from disease but also warfare, enslavement, and forced labor.
🎯 Exam TipThe Columbian Exchange appears constantly on AP World exams. Always explain BOTH directions and discuss disease separately — it was the single most devastating consequence for Indigenous peoples.
4.4Maritime Empires: Motives and Methods+

European maritime empires were built differently from land-based empires. Portugal and the Netherlands first established trading posts along coastlines. Spain went further, conquering the Aztec and Inca empires and establishing settler colonies in the Americas.

Key Vocabulary
Joint-Stock Company
A business where investors pool money to share risks and profits. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) and British East India Company were the most powerful.
Mercantilism
The economic theory that colonies exist to enrich the mother country by supplying raw materials and buying manufactured goods — exports greater than imports equals national wealth.
Encomienda System
A Spanish colonial system granting settlers the right to extract labor from Indigenous people in exchange for protecting and Christianizing them.
4.5The Atlantic Slave Trade+

The Atlantic slave trade was one of the greatest forced migrations in history. Between the 1500s and 1800s, approximately 12.5 million enslaved Africans were shipped to the Americas to work on plantations producing sugar, tobacco, cotton, and coffee for European markets.

Key Vocabulary
Middle Passage
The horrific transatlantic voyage enslaved Africans were forced to make, packed into ships under brutal conditions. An estimated 1.5–2 million people died during the crossing.
Triangular Trade
A three-part trading system: manufactured goods from Europe to Africa, enslaved people from Africa to the Americas, raw materials (sugar, tobacco) from Americas to Europe.
Plantation Economy
An agricultural system in the Americas based on large farms growing export crops using enslaved African labor — the economic engine of European colonialism.
4.6Internal and External Challenges+

Not everyone accepted European expansion quietly. Indigenous people resisted conquest. Enslaved Africans revolted and ran away. Some Europeans (like Bartolomé de las Casas) criticized the treatment of Indigenous people.

Key Vocabulary
Bartolomé de las Casas
A Spanish priest who witnessed atrocities against Indigenous people and wrote powerful critiques of the encomienda system, calling for their better treatment.
Maroon Societies
Communities formed by escaped enslaved Africans in remote areas of the Americas, who maintained African cultural traditions and resisted recapture.
4.7Changing Social Hierarchies+

European colonialism created entirely new social hierarchies based on race and ancestry. The Spanish in the Americas developed an elaborate casta system categorizing people by their mix of European, African, and Indigenous heritage.

Key Vocabulary
Casta System
A colonial racial hierarchy in Spanish America: Peninsulares at the top, then Creoles, Mestizos, Mulatos, Indigenous, and Africans.
Creoles
People of European descent born in the Americas. Despite their wealth, they ranked below Peninsulares — a frustration that later fueled independence movements in Unit 5.
4.8Unit 4 Continuity and Change+

What changed: new global trade networks, new racial hierarchies, biological exchange, and European maritime dominance. What continued: Asian empires remained powerful; most of Asia and Africa was not yet colonized; indigenous resistance continued throughout.

🎯 Exam TipUnit 4 is heavily tested. For DBQs about this period, always include: (1) the Columbian Exchange and disease, (2) the Atlantic slave trade, and (3) European motivations (God, Glory, Gold). Don't forget non-European perspectives!
Unit 5 · 12–15% of Exam

Revolutions

c. 1750–1900

⚖️ Exam weight: 12–15%

The period 1750–1900 was an age of revolutionary change — political, industrial, and social. The Enlightenment challenged old ideas about monarchy and religion. Political revolutions overthrew kings and created republics. The Industrial Revolution transformed how people worked and lived. These forces reshaped the entire world.

5.1The Enlightenment+

The Enlightenment was an 18th-century intellectual movement arguing that human reason — not tradition or religion — should guide society. Thinkers like Locke, Rousseau, and Voltaire questioned absolute monarchy, religious authority, and slavery. Their ideas directly inspired the American and French Revolutions.

Key Vocabulary
Enlightenment
An 18th-century European intellectual movement emphasizing reason, individual rights, and the social contract as the basis for good government.
John Locke
English philosopher who argued people have natural rights to life, liberty, and property — and that governments derive power from the consent of the governed.
Social Contract
The idea from Rousseau and Locke that governments are created by agreement between rulers and citizens — and can be overthrown if they violate that agreement.
5.2Political Revolutions+

Four major political revolutions transformed the world between 1750 and 1830: the American, French, Haitian, and Latin American revolutions. Each was influenced by Enlightenment ideas, but each had unique causes and outcomes.

Key Vocabulary
American Revolution (1776)
Colonists revolted against British rule, inspired by Enlightenment ideas. Created a republic — but initially excluded women, enslaved people, and non-property owners.
French Revolution (1789)
A radical revolution that abolished the monarchy, executed the king, and declared 'Liberty, Equality, Fraternity.' Led eventually to Napoleon Bonaparte's rise to power.
Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)
The only successful slave revolution in history. Enslaved Africans in Haiti overthrew French colonial rule, creating the first Black republic — inspiring abolitionists worldwide.
Latin American Independence
A wave of independence movements (1810–1830) led by Creole elites like Simón Bolívar, freeing most of Latin America from Spanish and Portuguese rule.
🎯 Exam TipThe Haitian Revolution is frequently under-studied but appears constantly on AP World exams. Know it well — it's the most radical revolution because it challenged BOTH colonial rule AND slavery simultaneously.
5.3The Industrial Revolution+

The Industrial Revolution began in Britain around 1760–1780 and spread globally. It transformed production from hand-made goods in homes to machine-made goods in factories. This created new social classes, new cities, and new problems including terrible working conditions and environmental pollution.

Key Vocabulary
Industrial Revolution
The shift from agricultural/handicraft economies to industrial manufacturing, powered first by coal and steam. Began in Britain due to coal resources, capital, and colonial markets.
Steam Engine
Invented by James Watt, the steam engine powered factories, locomotives, and ships — multiplying human productive capacity and enabling mass transportation.
Proletariat
Karl Marx's term for the industrial working class — factory workers who owned no property and sold their labor for wages, often in dangerous conditions.
Bourgeoisie
The middle class — factory owners, merchants, and professionals who owned the means of production and profited from industrial capitalism.
5.4Spread of Industrialization+

Industrialization spread from Britain to Belgium, France, Germany, and the United States by the mid-1800s. Some nations (Japan, Russia) industrialized under state leadership. Others (India, China) were prevented from industrializing by European colonial policies.

Key Vocabulary
Meiji Restoration (1868)
Japan's rapid modernization after American ships forced open Japanese ports in 1853. Japan adopted Western technology and industrialization to avoid colonization.
State-Led Industrialization
When governments (not private investors) drive industrialization — as in Russia under the tsars and Japan under the Meiji government, building railroads and factories directly.
5.5Technology in the Industrial Age+

Industrial technology changed everything. Railroads shrank distances. Telegraph wires connected continents instantly. Steel construction enabled skyscrapers and bridges. These technologies also gave industrial nations military advantages that enabled imperialism in Unit 6.

Key Vocabulary
Railroad
Steam-powered trains that could transport goods and people hundreds of miles cheaply and quickly — integrating national markets and enabling imperial expansion.
Telegraph
The first electrical communication technology, allowing instant long-distance messaging — revolutionizing business, journalism, and military coordination.
5.6Economic Effects of Industrialization+

Industrialization created capitalism as we know it — markets, banking, investment, corporations. It also created enormous inequality. Factory owners grew incredibly wealthy while workers lived in poverty. This gap inspired socialist and communist ideas challenging capitalism.

Key Vocabulary
Capitalism
An economic system where private individuals own the means of production and compete in markets for profit — the economic engine of industrialization.
Karl Marx
German philosopher who argued capitalism inevitably exploited workers and predicted a workers' revolution would create communism — a classless, stateless society.
Labor Unions
Organizations of workers who bargained collectively with employers for better wages, hours, and conditions — a response to terrible working conditions of early factories.
5.7Responses to Industrialization+

People responded to the problems of industrialization in many ways: forming unions, supporting socialist parties, pushing for reforms (child labor laws, voting rights), or advocating revolution. Women's suffrage movements also grew during this period.

Key Vocabulary
Socialism
An economic and political system where the community or state owns the means of production — a reform-based alternative to capitalism seeking to reduce inequality.
Women's Suffrage
The movement to give women the right to vote. New Zealand was first (1893). The movement grew as women's industrial and wartime contributions made their exclusion untenable.
Abolition Movement
The campaign to end slavery. Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery in 1833. The US abolished slavery in 1865.
5.8Social Effects of Industrialization+

Industrialization created new social structures. The middle class grew enormously. Urbanization pulled millions from countryside to city. New gender norms emerged — middle-class women were expected to stay home while men worked. Working-class women, however, worked in factories.

Key Vocabulary
Urbanization
The movement of people from rural areas to cities to work in factories. By 1900, more than half of Britain's population lived in cities — a dramatic change in human history.
Cult of Domesticity
A middle-class ideal that women's proper place was in the home as wives and mothers, while men worked in the public sphere — used to limit women's rights and opportunities.
5.9Unit 5 Wrap-Up: Causation in Revolutions+

The AP exam loves questions about causation in this unit. What caused political revolutions? Enlightenment ideas plus economic inequality plus colonial resentment. What caused industrialization? Available resources plus capital plus colonial markets. Practice explaining causes AND effects.

🎯 Exam TipConnect Units 5 and 6: Industrialization leads to more powerful militaries, which leads to more imperialism. This cause-and-effect chain is the backbone of the 1750–1900 period. Showing this connection demonstrates sophisticated historical thinking.
Unit 6 · 12–15% of Exam

Consequences of Industrialization

c. 1750–1900

⚖️ Exam weight: 12–15%

Industrialization gave European nations enormous military and economic power — and they used it to colonize much of the rest of the world. By 1900, European empires controlled about 80% of Earth's land surface. This unit examines how and why this happened, what resistance looked like, and how migration shifted populations globally.

6.1Rationales for Imperialism+

Why did European nations colonize so aggressively after 1850? Economic reasons (raw materials, new markets), political reasons (national competition, military bases), and ideological reasons (Social Darwinism, 'civilizing mission') all combined. These justifications masked the exploitation at the heart of imperialism.

Key Vocabulary
New Imperialism
The aggressive expansion of European empires into Africa, Asia, and the Pacific between 1870–1914, driven by industrial capitalism and nationalist competition.
Social Darwinism
The misapplication of Darwin's evolution to human societies — arguing Europeans were 'naturally superior' and therefore justified in ruling 'inferior' peoples. Pseudoscience used to justify racism.
White Man's Burden
A poem by Rudyard Kipling expressing the idea that Europeans had a duty to 'civilize' non-European peoples — hiding exploitation behind a paternalistic ideology.
6.2The Scramble for Africa+

At the 1884–85 Berlin Conference, European powers carved up Africa among themselves — without inviting a single African representative. Within 30 years, nearly all of Africa was colonized. Only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent.

Key Vocabulary
Berlin Conference (1884–85)
A meeting of European powers that formalized the 'Scramble for Africa,' establishing rules for colonization and dividing the continent — no Africans were consulted.
Battle of Adwa (1896)
Ethiopian forces under Emperor Menelik II defeated the Italian army, making Ethiopia one of the only African nations to successfully resist European colonization.
Indirect Rule
A British colonial strategy of governing through existing local leaders rather than replacing them entirely — cheaper but still exploitative.
6.3Indigenous Responses to Imperialism+

Colonized peoples were not passive victims — they resisted in many ways. Some organized armed resistance. Others used courts and petitions. Some accommodated to survive. The Sepoy Mutiny in India and the Boxer Rebellion in China are key examples of armed resistance.

Key Vocabulary
Sepoy Mutiny (1857)
A massive Indian uprising against British East India Company rule, triggered partly by rumors that rifle cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat (offensive to Hindus and Muslims).
Boxer Rebellion (1900)
A Chinese nationalist uprising against foreign influence and Christian missionaries, eventually crushed by an international military coalition — leading to even more foreign control of China.
Collaboration
When some local elites cooperated with colonial rulers to maintain their own power and status — a nuanced response that complicated simple narratives of resistance.
6.4Global Economic Developments+

Imperialism reshaped global economics. Colonies were forced to produce raw materials for European factories while buying European manufactured goods. This created dependency — colonial economies were structured to serve European needs, not local development.

Key Vocabulary
Cash Crop Economy
Colonial economies forced to grow single crops (cotton, rubber, sugar, coffee) for export rather than food crops — making colonies dependent and vulnerable to price changes.
Suez Canal (1869)
A canal through Egypt connecting the Mediterranean and Red Seas, dramatically shortening the route between Europe and Asia — a key strategic asset Britain eventually seized.
6.5Migration in the Industrial Age+

Industrialization and colonialism triggered massive migrations. Europeans migrated to the Americas and Australia. Indentured servants from India and China were shipped to British colonies after slavery was abolished. The Irish fled famine. Millions of people moved, reshaping populations worldwide.

Key Vocabulary
Indentured Servitude
A system replacing slavery after abolition — workers signed contracts to labor for several years in exchange for passage to a new country. Often exploitative in practice.
Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)
A US law banning Chinese immigration — a response to fear of Chinese workers competing with white workers, showing how migration triggered racist political backlash.
6.6Causes and Effects of Migration+

Migration had profound effects on both sending and receiving societies. It created multicultural societies in places like the Caribbean and Hawaii. It caused labor shortages in some regions and unemployment in others. Migrant communities often maintained their cultural traditions while adapting to new environments.

🎯 Exam TipMigration is often tested through documents showing different perspectives: a migrant's letter home, a government restriction law, an employer's contract. Practice analyzing these different viewpoints.
6.7Unit 6 Wrap-Up+

Unit 6 connects directly to Unit 7: imperialism created resentments, rivalries, and instabilities that exploded into World War I. The competition between European powers for colonies was one of the underlying causes of WWI. Understanding this connection is essential for sophisticated essay writing.

🎯 Exam TipUnits 5 and 6 together cover the most-tested period (1750–1900). Practice writing one LEQ comparing two different responses to industrialization or imperialism.
Unit 7 · 8–10% of Exam

Global Conflict

c. 1900–Present

⚖️ Exam weight: 8–10%

The 20th century was defined by catastrophic global wars. WWI (1914–1918) and WWII (1939–1945) killed tens of millions and reshaped political maps worldwide. This unit examines the causes, events, and consequences of these conflicts, as well as how war changed technology, society, and international relations.

7.1Causes of WWI+

Historians use the acronym MAIN to remember WWI's causes: Militarism, Alliance systems, Imperialism, and Nationalism. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914 was the spark — but the underlying tensions had been building for decades.

Key Vocabulary
MAIN (WWI causes)
Militarism (arms race), Alliances (Triple Alliance vs. Triple Entente), Imperialism (competition for colonies), Nationalism (ethnic tensions in empires).
Triple Alliance
The pre-WWI alliance between Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy — became the Central Powers (Italy switched sides).
Triple Entente
The pre-WWI alliance between France, Russia, and Britain — became the Allied Powers in WWI.
Trench Warfare
A combat strategy in WWI where armies dug parallel trenches facing each other, leading to years of stalemate and enormous casualties on the Western Front.
7.2WWI: Conduct and Consequences+

WWI introduced industrial-scale killing: machine guns, poison gas, tanks, and airplanes. Total war mobilized entire economies and societies. The war ended with the Treaty of Versailles (1919), which blamed Germany and set the stage for WWII.

Key Vocabulary
Total War
War that mobilizes an entire society — rationing food, drafting soldiers, redirecting industry. In WWI and WWII, entire economies and civilian populations were involved.
Treaty of Versailles (1919)
The peace treaty ending WWI that blamed Germany (War Guilt Clause), imposed massive reparations, stripped Germany of territory, and created the League of Nations.
Armenian Genocide (1915–1923)
The systematic mass murder of approximately 1.5 million Armenians by the Ottoman government during WWI — one of the first modern genocides.
League of Nations
An international organization created after WWI to maintain peace. The US never joined. It failed to prevent WWII but was the model for the United Nations.
🎯 Exam TipFor SAQs about the 20th century, practice identifying BOTH a cause AND an effect of WWI or WWII. The Treaty of Versailles leading to WWII is one of the most commonly tested cause-effect relationships.
7.3The Interwar Period (1919–1939)+

Between the wars, political extremism grew. Economic depression, wounded national pride, and fear of communism created fertile ground for fascism in Italy and Germany, and militarism in Japan. The Great Depression (1929) worsened everything.

Key Vocabulary
Fascism
A far-right political ideology emphasizing extreme nationalism, authoritarian government, and violent suppression of opposition. Emerged in Italy (Mussolini) and Germany (Hitler).
Great Depression (1929)
A global economic collapse beginning with the US stock market crash. Caused mass unemployment worldwide and destabilized democratic governments.
7.4Causes and Course of WWII+

WWII (1939–1945) was the deadliest conflict in human history, killing approximately 70–85 million people. It was truly global — fought across Europe, Africa, Asia, and the Pacific. The Holocaust was history's most horrific genocide.

Key Vocabulary
Holocaust
The Nazi regime's systematic genocide of six million Jews, plus millions of Roma, disabled people, and others. Industrial-scale murder using concentration camps.
Blitzkrieg
'Lightning war' — Germany's strategy of fast, overwhelming attacks using tanks and aircraft to overrun opponents before they could respond.
Atomic Bomb (1945)
The US dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945, forcing Japan's surrender and opening the nuclear age.
7.5Nationalist Movements During WWII+

WWII accelerated independence movements in colonized countries. Colonized peoples asked: if Europeans fought for freedom and democracy, why couldn't they have it too? Leaders like Gandhi in India and Ho Chi Minh in Vietnam mobilized their populations.

Key Vocabulary
Mahatma Gandhi
Leader of Indian independence who pioneered nonviolent civil disobedience (satyagraha) against British rule. India gained independence in 1947.
Atlantic Charter (1941)
A declaration by Roosevelt and Churchill supporting self-determination for all peoples — which colonized peoples pointed to as justification for their own independence.
7.6Economy in the Interwar Period+

The Great Depression caused mass unemployment globally and led governments to intervene in economies as never before. The US New Deal, Soviet Five-Year Plans, and Nazi Germany's rearmament all represented governments taking active control of economic planning.

Key Vocabulary
New Deal
FDR's series of US government programs responding to the Depression — creating jobs, regulating banks, and providing social safety nets.
Five-Year Plans
Stalin's centrally planned programs to rapidly industrialize the Soviet Union through collectivized agriculture and heavy industry — at enormous human cost.
7.7Technology and the World Wars+

Both world wars accelerated technological development. WWI introduced tanks, poison gas, and aircraft. WWII produced radar, jet planes, and the atomic bomb. Technologies developed for war were often later adapted for civilian use.

🎯 Exam TipWhen discussing WWII technology, the atomic bomb is the most important. Its use ended the war AND began the nuclear arms race of the Cold War — connecting Unit 7 directly to Unit 8.
7.8Mass Atrocities of the 20th Century+

The 20th century saw mass atrocities on an unprecedented scale: the Armenian Genocide, the Holocaust, Stalin's gulags, and the Cambodian genocide. The concept of 'crimes against humanity' and the United Nations were created partly in response to these horrors.

Key Vocabulary
Genocide
The deliberate killing of a large group of people, especially those of a particular ethnic group or nation. The term was coined after WWII to describe the Holocaust.
United Nations (1945)
An international organization created after WWII to prevent future wars, replace the failed League of Nations, and provide a forum for international diplomacy.
7.9Unit 7 Wrap-Up: Causation in Global Conflict+

The chain of causation from Units 6 to 7 to 8 is crucial: imperial competition leads to WWI, harsh peace leads to rise of fascism, which leads to WWII, which leads to the Cold War and decolonization. Understanding this chain helps you write sophisticated arguments on any essay about the 20th century.

Unit 8 · 8–10% of Exam

Cold War and Decolonization

c. 1900–Present

⚖️ Exam weight: 8–10%

After WWII, two superpowers — the US and the Soviet Union — competed for global influence in the Cold War. At the same time, colonized peoples across Africa and Asia won independence in a wave of decolonization. These two processes shaped the second half of the 20th century.

8.1Setting the Stage: Cold War Origins+

The US and USSR emerged from WWII as the world's two superpowers — but with completely opposite ideologies. The US championed capitalism and democracy. The USSR championed communism and state control. Their alliance against fascism collapsed almost immediately into rivalry.

Key Vocabulary
Cold War
The geopolitical rivalry between the US and USSR (1945–1991) fought through proxy wars, propaganda, arms races, and economic competition — never directly against each other.
Iron Curtain
Winston Churchill's term for the division between Western Europe (US-aligned, democratic) and Eastern Europe (Soviet-aligned, communist) after WWII.
Truman Doctrine (1947)
US policy committing to containing the spread of Soviet communism by supporting any country threatened by communist takeover — the basis of Cold War foreign policy.
8.2The Cold War+

The Cold War played out through an arms race, a space race, and proxy wars around the world. Both superpowers tried to spread their ideology while avoiding direct military confrontation — which could have meant nuclear war.

Key Vocabulary
NATO
The North Atlantic Treaty Organization — a military alliance among Western nations (led by the US) formed in 1949 to counter Soviet military power.
Warsaw Pact
The Soviet military alliance with Eastern European communist states — the Cold War counterpart to NATO.
Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)
A 13-day standoff when the US discovered Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba. The closest the world came to nuclear war — resolved through negotiation.
Nuclear Deterrence
The idea that neither side would launch nuclear weapons because both would be destroyed in retaliation — Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD).
8.3Effects of the Cold War+

The Cold War reshaped societies on both sides. The US experienced McCarthyism — a 'Red Scare' that suppressed civil liberties. The USSR maintained authoritarian control through secret police. Both superpowers poured enormous resources into military spending.

Key Vocabulary
McCarthyism
A period of anti-communist paranoia in the US (1950s) when Senator McCarthy accused thousands of Americans of being Soviet spies, often without evidence.
Space Race
The competition between the US and USSR to achieve superiority in space — from Sputnik satellite (1957) to the American moon landing (1969).
8.4Spread of Communism+

Communism spread to China (1949), North Korea, Vietnam, Cuba, and parts of Africa and Latin America. Each communist revolution had its own unique causes — many rooted in anti-colonial resentment rather than simply Soviet influence.

Key Vocabulary
Chinese Revolution (1949)
Mao Zedong's Communist Party defeated the Nationalist government, establishing the People's Republic of China — the world's most populous communist state.
Korean War (1950–1953)
A proxy war in which the US and UN fought to defend South Korea from North Korean and Chinese communist forces. Ended in a stalemate — still divided today.
Vietnam War (1955–1975)
A proxy war in which the US tried to prevent communist North Vietnam from taking South Vietnam. The US withdrew in 1973; Vietnam reunified under communism in 1975.
8.5Decolonization+

Between 1945 and 1975, dozens of new nations emerged from colonial rule in Asia and Africa. Some independence movements were peaceful (India), others violent (Algeria, Kenya). The Cold War often complicated decolonization — both superpowers tried to pull new nations into their sphere of influence.

Key Vocabulary
Indian Independence (1947)
Britain granted India independence but also partitioned it into Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan — causing massive religious violence and refugee crises.
Algerian War (1954–1962)
A brutal independence war in which Algeria won freedom from France after years of guerrilla warfare — a landmark in anti-colonial struggle.
Mau Mau Uprising
A Kenyan independence movement (1952–1960) that used armed resistance against British colonial rule, leading ultimately to Kenyan independence in 1963.
8.6Non-Aligned Movement and End of Cold War+

Many newly independent nations refused to align with either superpower, forming the Non-Aligned Movement. The Cold War ended when the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991 — exhausted by military spending, economic failures, and demands for freedom from Eastern Europeans.

Key Vocabulary
Non-Aligned Movement
A group of states (India, Yugoslavia, Egypt, and others) that chose independence from both NATO and the Soviet bloc during the Cold War — representing a 'third way.'
Fall of the Berlin Wall (1989)
The symbolic end of the Cold War — East Germans tore down the wall dividing Berlin, leading to German reunification and the collapse of Eastern European communism.
Dissolution of the USSR (1991)
The Soviet Union broke apart into 15 independent nations, ending the Cold War and leaving the US as the world's sole superpower — beginning the era of globalization.
🎯 Exam TipFor Unit 8, practice comparing different independence movements: peaceful vs. violent, Cold War-influenced vs. not. The Non-Aligned Movement is a great example of newly independent nations asserting agency rather than simply following a superpower.
Unit 9 · 8–10% of Exam

Globalization

c. 1900–Present

⚖️ Exam weight: 8–10%

After the Cold War, the world became more interconnected than ever — economically, culturally, and technologically. Globalization created enormous wealth but also widened inequality. This unit examines how technology, trade, migration, and culture have transformed the world in the late 20th and early 21st centuries.

9.1Advances in Technology and Exchange+

Technological advances after 1900 — especially the internet, container shipping, and commercial aviation — made the world dramatically smaller. Goods, ideas, and people now move across the globe faster and cheaper than ever before.

Key Vocabulary
Internet
A global network of computers enabling instant communication, commerce, and information sharing — the most transformative communication technology since the printing press.
Container Shipping
Standardized metal containers that revolutionized global trade by dramatically reducing the cost of shipping goods — making global supply chains possible.
Green Revolution
Agricultural innovations (high-yield crop varieties, fertilizers, irrigation) that dramatically increased food production in developing countries from the 1960s onward.
9.2Technological Advances and Disease+

Global connectivity also spread disease faster. HIV/AIDS spread globally in the 1980s. COVID-19 became a pandemic in 2020. But technology also helps fight disease — vaccines, antibiotics, and global health organizations like the WHO save millions of lives annually.

Key Vocabulary
HIV/AIDS Pandemic
A global health crisis beginning in the 1980s, disproportionately affecting sub-Saharan Africa. Highlighted global health inequality and the need for international cooperation.
World Health Organization
A UN agency coordinating international public health efforts — from eradicating smallpox to managing pandemics. An example of global governance in action.
9.3Debates About the Environment+

Industrial growth and population increases have created serious environmental problems: deforestation, pollution, and climate change. International agreements try to coordinate global responses — but conflicts between economic development and environmental protection remain unresolved.

Key Vocabulary
Climate Change
The long-term warming of Earth's climate due to human emissions of greenhouse gases — the defining environmental challenge of the 21st century.
Paris Agreement (2015)
An international treaty in which nearly all nations pledged to reduce carbon emissions to limit global temperature rise — a landmark in global environmental cooperation.
9.4Economics in the Global Age+

After the Cold War, capitalism became the dominant global economic system. International institutions like the WTO, IMF, and World Bank promoted free trade globally. But globalization has created winners and losers — some countries and workers benefited enormously while others were left behind.

Key Vocabulary
WTO
World Trade Organization — international body (est. 1995) that makes and enforces rules for global trade. Promotes free trade by reducing tariffs and other barriers.
Asian Tigers
South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore — nations that rapidly industrialized and achieved high-income status through export-led growth and state investment.
Neoliberalism
An economic ideology promoting free markets, deregulation, and privatization — the dominant economic philosophy of the late 20th century, championed by Reagan and Thatcher.
9.5Calls for Reform and Responses+

Globalization sparked both celebration and protest. Anti-globalization movements argued free trade harmed workers and the environment in poorer countries. Civil rights, feminist, and LGBTQ+ movements continued pushing for equality globally.

Key Vocabulary
Anti-Globalization Movement
A coalition of activists arguing that globalization primarily benefits corporations and wealthy nations while exploiting workers and damaging the environment in poorer countries.
Nelson Mandela
South African anti-apartheid leader imprisoned for 27 years who became South Africa's first democratic president (1994) — a global symbol of resistance and reconciliation.
9.6Globalized Culture+

Globalization has created a shared global culture — American movies, Korean pop music, Japanese anime, and Indian cuisine are consumed worldwide. But it has also triggered cultural resistance as communities try to protect local traditions from being overwhelmed.

Key Vocabulary
Cultural Homogenization
The fear that global culture (often American) is replacing local cultures — standardizing food, music, fashion, and values worldwide.
Cultural Hybridization
The blending of global and local cultures to create something new — like Bollywood (Indian plus Hollywood) or K-pop (Korean plus American pop music influences).
9.7Resistance to Globalization+

Not everyone embraces globalization. Religious fundamentalism, nationalism, and terrorism have emerged partly as reactions against global culture and US dominance. The September 11, 2001 attacks reshaped global security and launched the 'War on Terror.'

Key Vocabulary
Fundamentalism
A return to strict religious rules and rejection of modernism — found in Islam, Christianity, Hinduism, and other traditions. Often a reaction against globalization and cultural change.
September 11, 2001
Al-Qaeda terrorist attacks on the US that killed approximately 3,000 people and launched the US 'War on Terror' — reshaping global security and international relations.
International Criminal Court
An international tribunal established in 2002 to prosecute individuals for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity — a new form of global governance.
🎯 Exam TipUnit 9 connects to ALL previous units. Globalization echoes the trade networks of Unit 2, the colonialism of Unit 6, and the Cold War of Unit 8. Showing these connections on essays demonstrates the sophisticated historical thinking graders reward most.

Exam Prep Guide

Everything you need to know about the AP World History exam format — and how to score high

Exam at a Glance

Section I · Part A
Multiple Choice (MCQ)
55 questions · 55 minutes · 40% of score
MCQs come in sets of 3–4 questions tied to a primary or secondary source — a text, image, map, or chart. You need to read carefully and apply historical thinking, not just recall facts.
  1. Read the source first — identify author, date, context, and purpose
  2. Read the question carefully — note key words like "most likely," "primarily," "directly"
  3. Eliminate clearly wrong answers first — usually 2 are obviously off
  4. Pick the answer most supported by the document AND your knowledge
  5. Pace yourself — about 1 minute per question. Don't get stuck on one.
Section I · Part B
Short Answer (SAQ)
3 questions · 40 minutes · 20% of score
Each SAQ has 3 parts (a, b, c) worth 1 point each. Respond in 3–5 sentences per part — no thesis needed, just clear, specific answers with evidence.
  1. Read the verb: "Identify," "Explain," and "Describe" have different requirements
  2. Name a specific place or person — never say "various societies"
  3. Answer directly — no intro, no conclusion, just evidence and explanation
  4. Stay within the time period given — outside evidence usually doesn't count
  5. SAQ 3 is a choice — pick the time period you know best
Section II · Part A
Document-Based Question (DBQ)
1 question · 60 minutes · 25% of score
The DBQ gives you 7 documents and asks you to write an essay using them. You must argue a thesis, use evidence from the documents, source at least 3 using HAPP, add outside evidence, and contextualize.
  1. Use the 15-min reading period: group docs, draft thesis, plan HAPP moves
  2. Write a thesis that makes a defensible claim — not just a topic statement
  3. Use at least 6 of 7 documents as evidence in your essay
  4. Source 3+ documents using HAPP (Historical context, Audience, Purpose, Point of View)
  5. Add at least 1 piece of outside evidence not found in the documents
  6. Write a contextualization paragraph situating the topic historically
Section II · Part B
Long Essay (LEQ)
1 of 3 prompts · 40 minutes · 15% of score
The LEQ asks you to write an essay from memory arguing a historical claim. Choose one of three prompts (different time periods). You need: a thesis, contextualization, specific evidence, and historical reasoning.
  1. Choose the prompt for the time period you know best
  2. Spend 5 minutes outlining before you write — don't skip this
  3. Write a thesis in the intro that makes a clear, defensible argument
  4. Add contextualization — what happened BEFORE this period that sets the scene?
  5. Use at least 2 specific pieces of evidence in each body paragraph
  6. Apply the reasoning skill — if asked for causation, explain WHY things happened

How to Write a Strong Thesis

A good thesis does three things: makes a defensible claim, provides a line of reasoning, and goes beyond simply restating the prompt. It should be one or two sentences in your introduction.

❌ Weak: "The Industrial Revolution changed European society in many ways." (Too vague — anyone could write this.)

✅ Strong: "Although industrialization increased overall wealth, it primarily benefited the bourgeoisie while worsening conditions for the working class, ultimately leading to the rise of socialist movements that challenged capitalist inequality."

Formula: [Acknowledge complexity] + [Your main argument] + [because / which led to / by means of] + [line of reasoning / categories of analysis]

HAPP: How to Source Documents in the DBQ

To earn the sourcing point in the DBQ, explain how at least 3 documents' HAPP is relevant to your argument. Don't just identify it — explain how it affects the document's meaning and reliability.

Example: "Because Document 4 was written by a European missionary for a European Catholic audience, it likely exaggerates the disorder of non-Christian societies to justify continued missionary funding."

H
Historical Context
What was happening in the world at the time that influenced this document? Go beyond what's in the document itself.
A
Audience
Who was this document written FOR? How does that shape what the author says or leaves out?
P
Purpose
Why was this document created? To persuade? Inform? Celebrate? The purpose shapes its content and bias.
P
Point of View
What is the author's perspective or bias? How does their identity, role, or position shape what they wrote?

The 6 AP World History Themes

Every topic in AP World connects to at least one of these themes. Use them to organize your essays and spot patterns across units.

ENV — Environment
How humans shaped and were shaped by geography, climate, and natural resources
CDI — Culture
How beliefs, arts, and ideas spread and changed across cultures
GOV — Governance
How states formed, expanded, collapsed, and governed their people
ECN — Economics
How production, trade, and labor were organized across societies
SIO — Social
How class, race, gender, and family structured societies and interactions
TEC — Technology
How humans invented tools and how innovations spread and changed societies
